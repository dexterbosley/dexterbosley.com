On Shopping Agents

OCT 9, 2026
< BACK

I.

Much ado about whether consumers want shopping agents. I’m skeptical, but even granting the demand, the businesses that matter at scale have little incentive to support an independent agent shopping on the consumer’s behalf. They will protect their margins with their own agents or block outside ones.

II.

On its Q3 2026 earnings call Meta reported over 10m Muse setups in the US and Canada with about 25% active daily. Over a quarter of weekly users used it for shopping with average purchases above $200, including travel.

To model three-year business outcomes, let’s assume:

  • Twice as many weekly users as daily users: 2.5m daily → 5m weekly.
  • A quarter of those weekly users buy once a month at $200: $3bn in annual purchases.
  • A 5% take for Muse: equivalent to Stripe’s entire processing charge of roughly 3% plus a 2% convenience fee → $150m in annual gross fees.

The three scenarios below start at 5m weekly users and reach 10m, 50m and 200m after three years. For comparison, the dashed line applies ChatGPT’s earlier nine-month doubling pace to the same starting point, reaching 80m. The bull case assumes faster growth, comparable to ChatGPT’s later rise from 200m to nearly 700m weekly users in a year.

III.

In year three (so 2029-ish):

  • Bear, 26% annual user growth, loosely comparable to pre-IPO Duolingo pace (20% annual growth in monthly users): 10m weekly users × 25% buying monthly → $300m annual gross fees.
  • Base, 115% annual user growth, loosely comparable to early Snapchat pace (daily users roughly doubling in a year): 50m weekly users × 25% buying monthly → $1.5bn annual gross fees.
  • Bull, 242% annual user growth, roughly ChatGPT pace (weekly users grew from 200m to nearly 700m in a year): 200m weekly users × 50% buying twice monthly → $24bn annual gross fees.

The bull case assumes the faster ChatGPT benchmark plus twice the shopping rate and purchase frequency. By year three that means 100m shoppers spending $4,800 each annually through Muse: $480bn in gross merchandise value (GMV).

That’s about 5× DoorDash’s $102bn in 2025 marketplace gross order value. The $4,800 per shopper also exceeds the $4,280 average annual US ecommerce spend per shopper in Landmark Global’s 2026 factsheet.

At a 5% take (Stripe’s entire processing charge plus a 2% convenience fee) and zero assumed costs:

IV.

Now consider Amazon’s advertising business and Meta’s own ad business:

Revenue / increase
Muse bull case, year three (5% total take) $24bn
Amazon ad revenue increase, 2025 vs. 2024 $12.4bn
Amazon total ad revenue, 2025 $68.6bn
Meta ad revenue increase, 2025 vs. 2024 $35.5bn
Meta total ad revenue, 2025 $196.2bn

A 12.2% increase in Meta’s ad revenue would bring in $24bn a year, matching Muse’s most optimistic forecast. Improving ad targeting may be a better use of its scarce computing capacity than building Muse.

Amazon has a similar investment choice. It can put compute into agents that improve product discovery and ad targeting within its existing store. Advertising revenue can also fund lower prices. If Amazon devoted a quarter of that revenue to discounts after ad costs, it could cut prices by roughly 2%. An upstart must fund its computing costs and customer acquisition from the fees shoppers will pay for convenience.

V.

Travel is a legitimate consumer use case: people have a destination and dates and want help booking. Beyond that, b2c shopping agents bet on a consumer preference for productivity that feels absurd when the best consumer product of all time is an infinite feed of nonsense.

But even granting consumer demand, the businesses that matter at scale have little reason to support an independent agent serving the shopper. And the promised mom-and-pop insurgency need only look at Amazon to see who ends up in control.

The business incentives already make this a nonstarter to me. In 2026, we’re still in search of a broader consumer case.
