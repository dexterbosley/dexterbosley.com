Most users never change their default settings. This is not because they are lazy or uninformed. It is because defaults represent a recommendation from someone who presumably knows more about the software than they do.

---

This means the single most impactful product decision is often the least glamorous one: choosing the right default. A feature that is off by default might as well not exist for ninety percent of users. A setting buried three menus deep is functionally invisible.

The iPhone's success was largely a story of defaults. Out of the box, it worked. You didn't need to configure it. The defaults were so well-chosen that most people never opened the Settings app beyond connecting to WiFi.

---

When I evaluate software, I pay less attention to the feature list and more attention to the out-of-box experience. How does it work before I customize anything? If the answer is "poorly," then no amount of configurability will save it. The defaults are the product. Everything else is a footnote.
